Reinforcement Learning for Cube Reorientation and Continuous Rotation with a Simulated Dexterous Hand

A dexterous hand can bring a cube to the correct orientation and lose it a fraction of a second later. After assembling and calibrating a physical ORCA Hand, we defined a simulated +45° cube reorientation task and trained an initial policy with proximal policy optimization (PPO). Only four of 128 trials entered the 0.1 rad orientation tolerance, and none held it for 0.25 s. We aligned these brief entries with near misses and replayed the same initial states while changing individual joint targets or briefly suppressing individual finger contacts. These interventions showed how the timing of a contact could alter braking within one grasp. A hybrid controller that prepared the grasp for an announced sequence completed 478 of 512 reversal trials, up from 257 for the earlier controller. We then asked whether PPO could learn to brake across starting poses without a prescribed contact sequence.

For rotation about world X, we crossed two reward formulations, proximity to the target and improvement in orientation error, with two episode termination rules, first entry and 16 consecutive samples within tolerance. With equal training budgets and three training seeds, the proximity reward produced more first entries under both termination rules. Under that reward, delaying termination improved the number of trials that held for 0.25 s on their first entry from 0 to 102 of 576 in CPU PhysX evaluation. We then continued training from matched parent checkpoints, keeping the target active after entry and rewarding time spent within tolerance. On 192 fresh starts evaluated with GPU PhysX, the selected policy held the target throughout the final five seconds in 187 trials, compared with 39 for its matched reset control.

Extending the task to world Y exposed a different mechanism. Allowing forearm roll increased hand-supported terminal holds from 0 to 252 of 576 trials, although drops also increased from 70 to 274. At the first successful hold, the forearm contributed an average of 36.9° about Y and rotation within the palm contributed 7.6°. This distinction motivated a separate test of continuous Z rotation. Drawing on manual trials and the Aero Hand rotation task, we tilted the palm forward, fixed the wrist and forearm, and measured cube rotation relative to the palm.

For Z, reducing the penalty on command changes allowed successive fingers to take over contact as the cube turned. Increasing the drop penalty improved retention but sharply reduced trials that combined retention, rotation and repeated finger exchanges. Paired trajectories offered a more specific training signal. Half a second before a late drop, the cube had moved farther from its starting position in the palm than in a matched surviving trial, with median paired differences of 13.3 and 14.8 mm in two evaluation sets. We therefore penalized sustained outward slip while preserving the lower command-change penalty. Across three training seeds evaluated on the same 192 fresh starts, 25-second retention rose from 35.1% to 74.8%. Trials combining retention, a hand-supported turn through 45°, and at least two finger exchanges accompanied by both a command change and measured finger motion rose from 10.1% to 36.1%. Joint success varied from 37 to 133 of 192 starts across the three retrained policies. A next experiment will test whether a policy can brake near a requested angle and hold it after a sequence of finger exchanges.
